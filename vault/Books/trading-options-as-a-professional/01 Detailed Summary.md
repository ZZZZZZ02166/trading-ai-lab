## Introduction: Eight Essentials for Professional Options Trading

Bittman opens by identifying eight topics every professional options trader must master: option market fundamentals, option price behavior and the Greeks, synthetic relationships, pricing arbitrage strategies, volatility, delta-neutral trading, setting bid-ask prices, and managing position risk. These eight topics form the book's ten chapters. The progression is deliberate — each chapter builds on the last, and understanding arbitrage requires understanding synthetics, which requires understanding Greeks, which requires understanding price behavior.

This framing is important: Bittman is not writing a book about strategy selection. He is writing a book about *understanding what options do*, so that any strategy becomes analyzable.

## Chapter 1: Option Market Fundamentals

### The Seven-Part Option Order

Stock trades require four decisions (action, quantity, name, price). Option trades require seven: action (buy-to-open, sell-to-close, etc.), quantity in contracts, underlying, expiration month, strike price, option type, and price per share. Each additional dimension means an additional way to be right or wrong.

### Calls and Puts

A call gives the buyer the right to purchase the underlying at the strike; the seller has the obligation to sell. A put gives the buyer the right to sell; the seller has the obligation to buy. All four participants (call buyer, call seller, put buyer, put seller) have distinct P/L profiles.

### Moneyness, Intrinsic Value, Time Value

Options are characterized by moneyness: in-the-money (ITM), at-the-money (ATM), or out-of-the-money (OTM). ITM options have intrinsic value (the in-the-money amount). All options have time value (the remaining premium above intrinsic). A deep ITM call "trading at parity" has zero time value — its market price equals its intrinsic value.

### Markets and Market Makers

Public traders are individuals and organizations without exchange membership. Market makers are exchange members who quote both bids and offers simultaneously, providing liquidity. They earn the bid-ask spread in exchange for this service. The NBBO (National Best Bid and Offer) ensures public traders receive the best available price across all exchanges.

### Margin Requirements and Short Stock Rebate

Initial margin is the minimum equity required to open a marginable position. Maintenance margin is the minimum to keep it open. Short option positions face margin requirements because of assignment risk. Market makers who short stock receive a "short stock rebate" — 80% of the interest generated by the short proceeds. This rebate affects arbitrage strategy pricing significantly.

## Chapter 3: Basics of Option Price Behavior

### Five Pricing Components

Option prices are determined by five factors (analogous to insurance pricing):
1. **Stock price** (the asset)
2. **Strike price** (coverage level)
3. **Time to expiration** (coverage period)
4. **Volatility** (risk level — analogous to driving record)
5. **Interest rates and dividends** (carry cost)

Higher volatility always increases option value. Time decay is non-linear — options decay slowly far from expiration, then rapidly near expiration. This acceleration is most pronounced for ATM options.

### How Call and Put Values Change

Call values rise as stock price rises (positive delta). The relationship is curved — options are more sensitive to stock moves near the ATM strike (high gamma) than far away (low gamma). Put values fall as stock price rises (negative delta) with the same curvature property.

### Time Decay: Non-Linear and Complicated

A 100 Call at 30% vol declines from 6.53 at 90 days to 5.92 at 75 days to 5.25 at 60 days — relatively slowly at first. In the final two weeks, the rate accelerates dramatically. The percentage decay rate is similar across different starting option values, but the dollar decay is larger for higher-premium options.

For OTM options, higher volatility slows the rate of percentage decay in the early period. A 110 Call at 40% vol shows more even decay across its life than the same option at 20% vol (which decays mostly in the back half).

### Interest Rates and Dividends

Higher interest rates raise call values and lower put values (call holders avoid financing the stock; put holders delay receiving proceeds). Higher dividends have the opposite effect: they reduce call values and raise put values. These effects are most significant for deep ITM options and LEAPS.

### Three-Part Forecast: Direction, Timing, Volatility

The key insight of Chapter 3 is that option trading requires three simultaneous forecasts:
1. **Direction**: will the stock go up, down, or sideways?
2. **Timing**: will it happen before expiration?
3. **Volatility**: will implied volatility rise or fall?

Getting direction right but being wrong on timing or vol can still produce a loss. Joe's three-scenario analysis of Jumpco April 75 Calls illustrates this: each combination of direction × IV change × time produces a different P/L outcome.

## Chapter 4: The Greeks

This is the core chapter of the book — Bittman devotes more space to it than any other.

### Delta

Delta measures option price change per $1 stock move. Delta forms an S-curve: near zero for deep OTM calls, ~0.50 for ATM calls, near 1.00 for deep ITM calls. For puts: mirror S-curve from 0 to −1.00. Delta also approximates the probability of expiring ITM (rough heuristic).

Key behaviors:
- Time has minimal effect on delta away from the strike
- Higher volatility compresses all deltas toward 0.50
- Delta of deep ITM calls approaches 1.00 regardless of time or vol

### Gamma

Gamma measures how fast delta changes per $1 stock move. Gamma is highest for ATM options and lowest for deep ITM/OTM. The ATM gamma spikes exponentially as expiration approaches — in the final 20 days, gamma can become extremely large. Higher volatility reduces gamma for all options (the S-curve becomes more gradual with wider distribution).

### Vega

Vega measures option value change per 1% IV change. Vega is highest for ATM options and rises with time to expiry (LEAPS have most vega). Vega has a concave relationship with volatility itself — it peaks at moderate vol (~30–50%) then declines at extreme levels. This means the dollar impact of a 1% IV change is not constant across volatility regimes.

### Theta

Theta measures daily time decay (negative for long options, positive for short). ATM options have the most negative theta. Theta accelerates as expiration approaches — the final weeks cost more per day than earlier periods. Higher volatility increases theta dollar amounts. This is the gamma-theta linkage: the same factors that make gamma large also make theta large.

### Rho

Rho measures option value change per 1% interest rate change. Calls have positive rho; puts have negative rho. Rho is most significant for deep ITM long-dated options (LEAPS). In practice, rho matters for LEAPS and for arbitrage pricing of conversions and box spreads.

### Position Greeks

Position Greeks scale individual option Greeks to full position size:

| Greek | Calculation | What It Measures |
|-------|------------|-----------------|
| Position delta | option delta × contracts × 100 | Dollar directional exposure |
| Position gamma | option gamma × contracts × 100 | How delta will change per $1 move |
| Position vega | option vega × contracts × 100 | Dollar gain/loss per 1% IV change |
| Position theta | option theta × contracts × 100 | Dollar daily time decay income/cost |
| Position rho | option rho × contracts × 100 | Dollar gain/loss per 1% rate change |

Short options have negative position gamma and negative position vega. A 1% IV rise hurts short-vol positions; large moves hurt short-gamma positions. The compounding effect — when adverse stock moves coincide with IV expansion — is the most dangerous scenario for short option positions.

## Chapter 5: Synthetic Relationships

### Put-Call Parity

The fundamental equation: **+Call − Put = +Stock** (same strike, same expiry, interest rate ≈ 0).

Rearranging gives all six synthetic positions:
- Long stock = Long call + Short put
- Short stock = Short call + Long put
- Long call = Long stock + Long put (covered protective put)
- Long put = Short stock + Long call
- Short call = Short stock + Short put
- Short put = Long stock + Short call (**covered call**)

The covered call equals a synthetic short put — same risk profile, same P/L. Traders who sell covered calls for income are synthetically short puts with identical downside exposure.

### Time Value Equality

When interest rates are zero and there are no dividends, the time value of a call equals the time value of a put with the same strike. With non-zero rates, call time value exceeds put time value by the interest on the strike price (carry cost). This small difference affects pricing of synthetics for LEAPS and dividend-paying stocks.

### Effective Stock Price and Transaction Cost Comparison

When constructing a synthetic, the "effective" entry price depends on the net premium paid/received and the carry cost. In practice, traders choose whichever form (real or synthetic) is cheapest after transaction costs. Knowing the synthetic equivalent is a pricing check — it tells you whether the option you're looking at is fairly priced relative to its alternatives.

## Chapter 6: Arbitrage Strategies

### The Conversion

Conversion = long stock + long put + short call (same strike, same expiry). Net delta ≈ 0. P/L = put-call parity discrepancy. Market makers use conversions to lock in riskless profits when put-call parity is violated. The target call price formula: strike + (interest × days/365) + put price − stock price. If the actual call trades above this, a profitable conversion exists.

### The Reverse Conversion

Reverse conversion = short stock + short put + long call. Mirror image of conversion. Market makers with high short stock rebates find more reverse conversions viable. Both conversions and reverse conversions carry **pin risk**: if stock closes exactly at the strike at expiration, the market maker doesn't know if they'll be assigned, creating unexpected overnight stock exposure.

### Box Spreads

Box = bull call spread + bear put spread (same strikes, same expiry). Always worth exactly the spread width at expiration (e.g., a 90–100 box is always worth $10). Present value = spread width − interest. If box trades below PV → buying locks in riskless profit. If above PV → selling creates profit. Box spreads are pure interest-rate instruments: no directional, gamma, or vega exposure. Large institutions use them as synthetic money market instruments.

## Chapter 7: Volatility

### Historical vs. Implied Volatility

Historical (realized) volatility: backward-looking annualized standard deviation of returns. Implied volatility: the volatility that equates a pricing model's output to the observed market price — forward-looking, market consensus.

Example: Gary calculates a 100 Call's theoretical value at 26% vol = $2.57. Market trades it at $3.40. Back-solving: implied vol = 32.84%. The market is pricing in more uncertainty than Gary assumed.

### Normal Distribution and Expected Moves

Stock returns are approximately normally distributed. The 1σ expected move over time T = IV × stock price × √(T/252). A $100 stock at 32% IV has a 68% probability of staying within ±$32 over one year (±1σ). The distribution screen in Op-Eval Pro calculates these ranges for user-specified horizons.

### Volatility and Stock Prices: Inverse Relationship

Stock price drops tend to coincide with IV spikes. The 2007–2008 example shows stock price declining from $115 to $90 while IV spiked from ~18% to ~35%. This inverse relationship means long-put positions (which are long vega) are doubly effective as hedges: they profit from the stock decline and from the IV expansion.

### Volatility Skew

Equity options show a persistent skew: OTM puts have higher IV than equidistant OTM calls (downward-sloping IV vs. strike curve). Causes: persistent demand for downside protection; asymmetric fear of crashes. A 90-strike put may trade 8+ vol points above a 110-strike call. This creates opportunity in risk reversals.

## Chapter 8: Delta-Neutral Trading

### Two Types of Delta-Neutral Traders

1. **Speculators**: use IV-vs-realized-vol forecasts to express a view; hold positions days to weeks; assume the risk their forecast is wrong.
2. **Market makers**: use delta-neutral hedging as a short-term technique to close positions at bid or ask before IV changes adversely; typically hold for minutes to hours.

### Long Delta-Neutral (Long Gamma)

Long options + short stock = net delta ≈ 0. Profits if:
- Realized vol exceeds implied vol (gamma scalps accumulate)
- OR IV rises (vega gain)

Gamma scalping mechanism: as stock rises, delta increases → sell stock to re-neutralize (locks in small gain). As stock falls, delta decreases → buy stock to re-neutralize (another small gain). Each round-trip scalp profits. Total gamma P/L = sum of all scalps.

Breakeven condition: total gamma scalp profits ≥ total theta paid.

### Short Delta-Neutral (Short Gamma)

Short options + long stock = net delta ≈ 0. Profits if:
- Realized vol is below implied vol (theta > gamma losses)
- OR IV falls (vega gain on top)

Risk: a sudden large move generates gamma losses that may exceed all theta collected. Requires active rebalancing to prevent directional exposure from accumulating.

### Rebalancing Frequency

Too frequent → transaction costs consume all scalp profits. Too rare → large directional exposure defeats the purpose. Professional rule: rebalance when delta drifts by approximately 1 standard deviation of the expected daily move.

## Chapter 9: Setting Bid and Ask Prices

### Theory of the Bid-Ask Spread

Market makers earn profit by buying at the bid and selling at the ask. They delta-hedge immediately to neutralize direction. The bid-ask spread is the edge they earn for providing liquidity. Example: sell 10 calls at bid 1.85, buy 460 shares to hedge. Stock rises to $55. Buy back calls at ask 1.85, sell stock at $55. The stock gain is the market maker's profit from the spread trade.

In adverse scenarios, the delta hedge partially offsets call losses but may not fully recover the bid-ask concession. The key is to close positions before conditions change enough to erode the edge.

### Pricing Formulas for Conversions and Box Spreads

Conversion target call: strike + (interest × days/365) + put − stock  
Reverse conversion target call: stock − put + (interest × days/365)  
Box spread fair value: (high − low strike) × [1 − (interest × days/365)]

These formulas tell market makers exactly what price they need to execute a profitable arbitrage. Two-trade strategies can capture the edge on each leg separately.

## Chapter 10: Managing Position Risk

### Position Greeks for a Multi-Leg Portfolio

Position Greeks are additive: the total position delta/gamma/vega/theta is the sum across all legs. Example: 20 long 70–75 call spreads have:
- Position delta = 546 (peaks between strikes)
- Position gamma = 20 (positive below lower strike, negative above upper)
- Position vega = 42 (bell-shaped positive between strikes)
- Position theta = +170/day (most positive between strikes)

### How Greeks Change as Stock Moves

A bull call spread's gamma changes sign as stock crosses the short-call strike. Below lower strike: positive gamma. Above upper strike: negative gamma. Traders who don't track this flip can be surprised by their position becoming "short gamma" after a strong move.

### Setting Dollar-Denominated Risk Limits

Risk limits should be in dollar terms: max acceptable loss per $1 adverse move (delta), per large move (gamma), per 1% IV change (vega), per day (theta). These translate directly into stop-loss levels and position sizing constraints.

### The Professional Edge: Combined Greek Understanding

Bittman's conclusion: professionals who understand how all five Greeks interact — not just individually — have a decisive edge. A position can appear safe on delta but dangerous on gamma; safe on theta but exposed to vega. Only by evaluating the full Greek profile across realistic scenarios does a trader truly understand what they own.
