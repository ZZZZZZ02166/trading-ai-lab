# Chapter 1: Economics of Option Market Making
*pp. 1–8 | Option Market Making, Allen Jan Baird*

---

## Summary

This chapter establishes the foundational economics of the market maker's role. A market maker is a professional who stands ready to buy at a bid and sell at an offer at any time — providing the crucial service of *immediacy*. Without market makers, buy and sell orders would rarely be matched simultaneously, making prices far more volatile.

The market maker earns the bid/offer spread (the "edge") as gross profit. Market-making is viewed through a supply/demand lens: when prices rise above long-term equilibrium, market makers sell to bring prices back; when prices fall below, they buy. The spread around equilibrium (asked P1 above, bid P2 below) is the market maker's compensation.

Options/futures trading is a zero-sum game — not wealth-creating, but wealth-redistributing through risk shifting. This is socially useful: it allows commercial firms to offload price risk and focus on productive activity.

---

## Key Concepts

- **Immediacy:** The service of always being available to trade at a price
- **Bid/offer spread (the edge):** Primary income source for market makers
- **Risk-neutral trading:** Profit from the spread, not from directional bets
- **Carryover position:** Accumulated multi-strike, multi-expiration options inventory — the central management challenge

---

## Key Lessons

1. **Net profit per trade is well below the full spread.** Studies of securities dealers and futures scalpers show market makers cannot capture the full bid/offer spread on every trade. Overhead, competition for order flow, and edge giveaways mean net profits are thin — requiring high volume.

2. **Option market making differs fundamentally from stock/bond dealing.** Stock dealers can go flat at end of day. Option market makers accumulate carryover positions across strikes and expirations that cannot be quickly liquidated. This carryover risk — responding to five independent variables — is what makes option market making uniquely challenging.

3. **Pure market making is risk-neutral.** Profit comes from liquidity provision, not directional views. Market makers who speculate on direction risk catastrophic losses because options are highly leveraged with nonlinear payoffs.

4. **Limited-risk strategies are safer for carryover.** Not all option positions are catastrophically exposed. Understanding which strategies are limited-risk (maximum loss is defined) vs unlimited-risk is essential for long-term survival.

## Warnings

- The 1980s witnessed multiple high-profile bankruptcies of option trading firms (Volume Investors, Fossett, Stotler) that failed to manage carryover risk.
- Market makers who take directional "shots" are speculating — this is incompatible with the pure market-making philosophy and will eventually lead to large losses.
